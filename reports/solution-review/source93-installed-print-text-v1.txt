Calculate (−3)2 , −3 2 , (−2)3 , ∣ − 2 − 3∣, and ∣2 − (−3)∣. Write the multiplication or subtraction before
giving the answer. Explain which two expressions measure a distance.

Review: Algebra warm-up

  Show hint
A power acts on its bracketed base. Without brackets, the power is evaluated before a leading minus.
Distance is the absolute value of the difference between two positions.

  Show worked solution


  Step 1: Identify the base of each power. In (−3)2 , the base is the whole number −3 . Squaring it
  means multiplying two copies:



                                        (−3)2 = (−3)(−3) = 9.


  The product of two negative numbers is positive. In −3 2 , the base of the power is 3 ; the leading minus
  is applied afterwards. Thus −3 2 = −(3 ⋅ 3) = −9 . The brackets change the order of operations, so
  these two expressions have different values.

  Step 2: Expand the odd power. Three copies of −2 give (−2)3 = (−2)(−2)(−2). Multiply the first
  two to obtain 4 , then multiply 4 by −2 to obtain −8 . An odd number of negative factors leaves a
  negative product.

  Step 3: Perform subtraction before taking absolute value. First, −2 − 3 = −5 , so ∣ − 2 − 3∣ = ∣ −
  5∣ = 5 . Second, subtracting a negative adds its positive opposite: 2 − (−3) = 2 + 3 = 5 , so ∣2 −
  (−3)∣ = ∣5∣ = 5 . Absolute value measures how far a number lies from zero; that distance cannot be
  negative.

  Step 4: Interpret the two distances. ∣ − 2 − 3∣ is the distance between positions −2 and 3 , and ∣2 −
  (−3)∣ is the distance between 2 and −3 . Each interval has length 5 . Swapping the two positions
  changes the sign of their difference but preserves its absolute value. This is the scalar distance used in
  later error and contraction bounds.

  Exercise 0.W4 — Easy: Solve an inequality without losing its direction


Solve −3x + 2 ≤ 8 over the reals and write the answer as an interval. Then solve 1 ≤ 2x + 3 < 7 .
Check an endpoint, an interior point and an excluded point for each answer.

Review: Algebra warm-up

  Show hint
Subtracting the same number preserves order. Dividing by a negative number reverses order. A double
inequality imposes two conditions at once.

  Show worked solution


  Step 1: Remove the constant term. Subtract 2 from both sides of −3x + 2 ≤ 8 , obtaining −3x ≤ 6 .

  Step 2: Divide by the coefficient and account for its sign. The coefficient −3 is negative. Division
  reverses the inequality, so x ≥ 6/(−3) = −2 . The complete solution set is [−2, ∞). Its left
  endpoint is included because the original comparison allows equality; infinity is never an included real
  endpoint.

  Step 3: Verify three inputs. At x = −2 , the original left side is 6 + 2 = 8 , so the endpoint works. At
  x = 0 , it is 2 ≤ 8 , so this interior point works. At x = −3 , it is 9 + 2 = 11 > 8 , so this excluded
  point fails. Had we kept the wrong direction when dividing, these checks would reveal it.

  Step 4: Solve both sides of the double inequality. Subtract 3 from all three expressions to obtain
  −2 ≤ 2x < 4 . Divide by the positive number 2 , preserving both directions: −1 ≤ x < 2 . The
  solution interval is [−1, 2).

  Step 5: Check both endpoint types. At x = −1 , 2x + 3 = 1 , so the non-strict lower condition holds.
  At x = 0 , the value is 3 , between 1 and 7 . At x = 2 , the value is 7 , so the strict upper condition fails.
  The different brackets record different requirements.

Statements and implications


   DEFINITION — CONNECTIVES, CONVERSE, CONTRAPOSITIVE

   For statements P , Q (each true or false): ¬P (not P ), P ∧ Q (and), P ∨ Q (or, including
   both), P ⇒ Q (if P then Q; false only when P is true and Q false), and P ⇔ Q (both
   directions). The converse of P ⇒ Q is Q ⇒ P ; its contrapositive is ¬Q ⇒ ¬P . An
   implication is equivalent to its contrapositive and to ¬P ∨ Q, but not to its converse. "P is
   sufficient for Q" means P ⇒ Q; "P is necessary for Q" means Q ⇒ P .


Intuition and example. An implication is a promise, broken only when P holds and Q fails; if P
never holds it is vacuously true, so a condition checked on an empty set certifies nothing. "x >
2 ⇒ x2 > 4" is true; its converse is false (x = −3); its contrapositive "x2 ≤ 4 ⇒ x ≤ 2" is true.
Module 14 requires (D(ξ) ≤ 0 ∧ fcl (ξ) ∈ B) ∨ V (ξ) ≥ ρ for all ξ ∈ B, with D(ξ) ≤ 0 a
                                              ​




decrease condition on V (the module writes F ). Since P ∨ Q ≡ (¬Q ⇒ P ): whenever V (ξ) <
ρ, V decreases and the successor fcl (ξ) stays in B.

   COMMON MISTAKE — READING A CERTIFICATE BACKWARDS

   Certificates are sufficient conditions ("the LMI is feasible ⇒ the loop is stable"), and a failed
   certificate proves nothing: "infeasible ⇒ unstable" is the inverse ¬P ⇒ ¬Q, which is
   equivalent to the converse, not to the statement. The S-procedure of Module 2 is special
   because, for one constraint (and a strictly feasible point), its sufficient condition is also
   necessary.



Quantifiers, negation, and why their order matters


   DEFINITION — QUANTIFIERS AND THEIR NEGATION

   "∀x ∈ X : P (x)" (for all) is true iff every element of X satisfies P ; it is vacuously true if
   X = ∅. "∃x ∈ X : P (x)" (there exists) is true iff at least one element does. Negation
   swaps them:



    ¬(∀x ∈ X : P (x)) ⟺ ∃x ∈ X : ¬P (x),                      ¬(∃x ∈ X : P (x)) ⟺ ∀x ∈ X


   To negate a chain of quantifiers, swap every ∀ ↔ ∃ and negate the innermost statement. In
   set language, "for every i" is an intersection and "for some i" a union.
Intuition. In "∀x ∃y : P (x, y)" you may answer each x with its own y; "∃y ∀x : P (x, y)" forces
one y before seeing x. Committing first is harder: ∃y ∀x P implies ∀x ∃y P , but not conversely.
Over R, "∀x ∃y : y > x" is true (answer y = x + 1), while "∃y ∀x : y > x" claims a largest real
number and is false.

  Exercise 0.GS1 — Easy: Separate an element from a set of elements


Let A = {1, 2} and B = {{1}, 2} . Decide whether 1 ∈ A, {1} ⊆ A, {1} ∈ A, {1} ∈ B , and ∅ ⊆ A.
Find ∣A∣ and ∣B∣. Explain each test in words.

Review: Sets and logic

  Show hint
Membership tests one listed object. A subset test examines the elements inside the proposed subset. The
number 1 and the set {1} are different objects.

  Show worked solution

  Step 1: List the objects in each set. A contains the numbers 1 and 2 . B contains the singleton set
  {1} as one object and the number 2 as another. Braces create a set; they are part of the object being
  described.

  Step 2: Test membership. 1 ∈ A is true because the number 1 is listed. {1} ∈ A is false because the
  singleton set is not either listed number. {1} ∈ B is true because that singleton set is explicitly one of
  B 's two objects.

  Step 3: Test a subset. {1} ⊆ A means that every member of {1} belongs to A. Its only member is
  the number 1 , and 1 ∈ A, so this statement is true. It does not ask whether the singleton itself is listed
  in A.

  Step 4: Handle the empty set. ∅ ⊆ A means that every element of the empty set belongs to A. There
  is no element that could fail this test. Formally, a failure would require an x ∈ ∅ with x /
                                                                                             ∈ A, and such
  an x does not exist. The subset statement is therefore true; this is called a vacuously true universal
  statement.

  Step 5: Count distinct elements. Both ∣A∣ and ∣B∣ are 2 . The singleton in B counts as one element of
  B ; we do not open it and count its contents again. Vertical bars around a finite set mean cardinality,
  whereas vertical bars around a real number mean absolute value. The type of the object determines

One fixed input for all states. If such an input existed, the allowed state x = 1 would force 1 + u = 0
, hence u = −1 . The other allowed state x = −1 would force −1 + u = 0 , hence u = 1 . A single
real number cannot equal both −1 and 1 . Thus no a ≥ 0 , however large, makes this stronger
statement true. Increasing the input interval can provide more state-dependent choices; it cannot make
the uniquely required values equal.

Check a small actuator range directly. At a = 1/2 , the state x = 1/4 can be cancelled by u =
−1/4 , which lies in [−1/2, 1/2]. But x = 1 needs u = −1 , outside that interval. Thus one
successful interior-state check does not prove the universal claim. At the limiting value a = 1 , every
required −x is available, including the endpoint commands. The parameter threshold arises from
actuator feasibility, while the impossibility of a shared command arises from incompatible equations;
these are separate reasons for failure.

  Show hint

   COMMON MISTAKES IN PROOFS

   (1) Circular reasoning: assuming the trajectory stays where a certificate holds in order to prove

     includes the time as well as the state. Revisiting the same state at another time is then insufficient to
     force the same successor. In a finite state-and-mode model, a repetition argument would need to count
     the combined states instead.

   DEFINITION — MEASURE ZERO, A.E., A.S., ESSENTIAL SUPREMUM

   N ⊆ Rn has measure zero (zero volume) if for every ε > 0 it can be covered by countably
   many boxes of total volume below ε, e.g. finite sets in positive dimension, lines in R2 ,
   hyperplanes {x : w ⊤ x + b = 0} with w =    0, and finite unions of these. A property holds
   almost everywhere (a.e.) if it fails only on a measure-zero set, and an event holds almost
   surely (a.s.) if its probability is 1. The essential supremum is the smallest almost-sure upper
   bound, ess sup Z = inf{c : P(Z ≤ c) = 1}.


Intuition and example. A ReLU network is differentiable except at the kinks where a hidden unit
switches: finitely many pieces of hyperplanes w ⊤ x + b = 0 (whole hyperplanes for one hidden
layer), a measure-zero set. Every Lipschitz function on Rn is differentiable a.e. (Rademacher). If U
is uniform on [0, 1] and Z = U except Z = 10 when U = 12 , then ess sup Z = 1, although the
                                                               ​




supremum over all outcomes is 10. Almost-sure safety tolerates failures on a probability-zero set
of disturbances; robust safety tolerates none (Primer C). Used in Module 1 (a.s. safety, ess sup),
Module 12 and Module 10 (differentiable a.e.), Module 13 (gradient norm 1 almost everywhere) and
Module 8 (a.s. convergence).


   PITFALL — "PROBABILITY ZERO" IS NOT "IMPOSSIBLE"

   A statement for almost every x says nothing about a particular x, and the exceptions may sit
   exactly where it matters (a boundary, a ReLU kink). Upgrading needs structure such as
   continuity: a continuous function that is ≥ 0 almost everywhere is ≥ 0 everywhere.

Uniform continuity, nets, and gridding with a Lipschitz margin

f is uniformly continuous on X if ∀ε > 0 ∃δ > 0 ∀x, y ∈ X : ∥x − y∥ < δ ⇒ ∣f(x) −
f(y)∣ < ε: one δ for all points (for continuity δ may depend on the point). Continuous functions on
compact sets are uniformly continuous (Heine–Cantor), and L-Lipschitz ones are too, with δ =
ε/L for L > 0 (any δ works if L = 0: f is constant); 1/x on (0, 1] and x2 on R are continuous
but not uniformly so.


   DEFINITION AND FACT — Τ -NETS, COVERING NUMBERS, A LIPSCHITZ MARGIN

   For τ > 0, a finite Z ⊆ X is a τ -net if every x ∈ X has some z ∈ Z with ∥x − z∥ ≤ τ ;
   the covering number M(τ, X) is the smallest size of one. For d ≥ 1, cubes of side 2τ/                         d
                                                                                                                 ​




   have half-diagonal τ , so M(τ, [0, 1]d ) ≤ ⌈ d/(2τ)⌉d : for τ = 0.1, 64 points in d = 2 but
                                                                ​




   1610 ≈ 1.1 ⋅ 1012 in d = 10. Fact: if h is L-Lipschitz, Z is a τ -net of X and h(z) ≥ Lτ on
   Z , then h ≥ 0 on X , because h(x) ≥ h(z) − L∥x − z∥ ≥ Lτ − Lτ = 0. ■

Connectedness, semicontinuity and density

Connectedness. A is path-connected if any two points are joined by a continuous path inside A;
path components are the maximal path-connected subsets (for open sets they coincide with the
usual connected components, in general not). Continuous images of path-connected sets are path-
connected, so a continuous parameterisation from RN reaches only one path component of a set
that has several. A finite grid in Euclidean space has no continuous path joining distinct points; its
connectivity depends on an adjacency rule, and certified reachability is a third notion (Module 6,
Module 4, Module 13, Module 11).

Semicontinuity. f is lower semicontinuous if f(x) ≤ lim inf t f(xt ) whenever xt → x,
                                                                ​     ​              ​




equivalently if every {f ≤ c} is closed; the Weierstrass argument still yields minima on nonempty
compact sets. Example: f = 0 on (−∞, 0], f = 1 on (0, ∞) (Module 8).

Density. A class G is dense in F in the sup norm on a compact X if ∀f ∈ F ∀ε > 0 ∃g ∈ G :
supX ∣f − g∣ ≤ ε: arbitrarily good approximation, not exact representation (Module 13).

  Show hint
Open means each member has a small interval around it contained in the set. Closed means all limits of
convergent sequences of members are retained, or equivalently the complement is open.

  Show worked solution

  Step 1: Test openness of [0, 1). The number 0 is a member. For any radius r > 0 , the neighbourhood
  (−r, r) contains −r/2 , which is outside [0, 1). Thus 0 has no neighbourhood contained in the set,
  and the set is not open.

  Step 2: Test closedness independently. For integers n ≥ 1 , the numbers xn = 1 − 1/n all lie in
                                                                                  ​




  [0, 1). They converge to 1 , since ∣xn − 1∣ = 1/n → 0 . But 1 is excluded, so the set is not closed
                                        ​




  either. It is therefore neither open nor closed; the two properties are not logical opposites.

  Step 3: Check the entire ambient space. For any x ∈ R, the interval (x − 1, x + 1) stays in R, so R

  DEFINITION — POLYNOMIAL TIME, P, NP, NP-HARD (WORKING VERSION)

  A decision problem asks a yes/no question about an input of n bits; it is in P if one algorithm
  answers every instance in O(nk ) steps for a fixed k . NP contains the problems whose "yes"
  answers have certificates checkable in polynomial time (a claimed counterexample to
  robustness is checked by one forward pass). A problem is NP-hard if every NP problem
  reduces to it in polynomial time, and NP-complete if it is also in NP. Whether P = NP is open;
  if P =
        NP, no polynomial-time algorithm solves all instances of an NP-hard problem. It is a
  worst-case statement about growing sizes, not a claim that every instance is hard; it is why
  the modules use relaxations (LipSDP) and branch-and-bound verifiers.

From the mathematics to a real decision
A useful mathematical answer changes what someone can responsibly do. A temperature reading
may support accepting a chamber, choosing a cooling command, or waiting for another solver
iteration. Those are different decisions, and they require different statements. This chapter follows
a constructed thermal-chamber model from a single reading to a claim about every future step. The
numbers are teaching choices, not measurements from a particular machine. Their purpose is to
make the order of the reasoning visible.
   LEARNING OBJECTIVES


      Translate an acceptance rule into containment of sets and explain what a failed test
      means.

      Place control choices and unknown disturbances in the correct quantifier order.

      Build an all-time conclusion from a one-step bound without assuming the conclusion.

      Turn an error recurrence into a finite iteration budget, including its unavoidable error floor.



Begin with the decision and its units
Let x be temperature above a chosen reference, measured in degrees Celsius, and let the allowed
interval be C = [0, 5]. This is a specification: for this hypothetical task a temperature deviation in
that interval is acceptable. It is not a claim that the interval is physically appropriate for any actual
chamber. A sensor reading y comes with a deterministic error bound ∣x − y∣ ≤ ε. The word
deterministic means that every error in the interval is permitted; no probability distribution has been
assumed.

The possible true values form I(y) = [y − ε, y + ε]. Acceptance follows when I(y) ⊆ C . Notice
the change from a statement about one number to a statement about all numbers compatible with
the information. Checking only y ∈ C answers whether the displayed reading is acceptable. It does
not answer whether the unknown temperature is acceptable. If containment fails, the information
allows an unacceptable temperature; it may also allow acceptable temperatures. The correct
conclusion is that this acceptance test has not certified the chamber.

This distinction is useful whenever a calculation is an upper or lower bound rather than an exact
observation. Write down what the bound encloses before deciding what its sign proves. A positive
upper bound on an error does not mean that this error occurred. A negative lower bound on a safety
margin does not mean the actual margin is negative. Both statements describe the limits of the
information.

Mathematical punctuation is recording a physical timing assumption.


  Worked example — A command interval at one measured temperature                                      ▸


  Decision. At x = 4.5 degrees Celsius, find every command that keeps the next step in C , whatever
  allowed warming occurs. Since the update increases with w , its complete range is [4.95 +
  u, 5.35 + u]. There is no need to test infinitely many disturbances separately: the two endpoints
  bound every intermediate value.

  Translate containment. The lower endpoint must be at least zero and the upper endpoint at most
  five. Thus u ≥ −4.95 and u ≤ −0.35. Intersect these conditions with the actuator interval [−1, 0]
  to obtain u ∈ [−1, −0.35]. The intersection matters: a mathematically useful command is
  irrelevant if the actuator cannot issue it.

  Select and check. Choose u = −0.5. The successor interval is [4.45, 4.85], contained in [0, 5].
  The nominal disturbance w = 0 would predict 4.45; accounting for every allowed disturbance
  raises the upper prediction to 4.85. This verifies one step from the stated temperature. It has not yet
  verified a whole trajectory or a chamber with unmodeled delays.


To cover every x ∈ C , one explicit choice is u(x) = −0.18x. This command lies in [−0.9, 0] ⊆
[−1, 0], and the resulting successor interval is [0.92x, 0.92x + 0.4]. Its lower endpoint is
nonnegative; its upper endpoint is at most 0.92(5) + 0.4 = 5. We have supplied a witness for the
existential quantifier at every state, using one formula rather than infinitely many separate
calculations.

No single fixed command works for the entire interval. At x = 0, allowing w = 0 forces u ≥ 0,
hence u = 0. At x = 5, allowing w = 0.4 forces u ≤ −0.9. These requirements conflict.
Feedback changes the available decision because the command may depend on the observed
temperature. This conclusion follows from the two boundary states, not from how many points
were simulated.


Extend the claim one step at a time

Suppose the same update and disturbance bounds apply at every integer time t ≥ 0, and apply the
feedback ut = −0.18xt . To prove xt ∈ C for all time, first state the base case x0 ∈ C . Next
             ​              ​                ​                                            ​




assume only that the current xt lies in C . The one-step calculation then shows xt+1 ∈ C for every
                                     ​                                                        ​




permitted wt . Induction completes the argument. It permits disturbances to change arbitrarily from
                 ​




step to step, because the one-step result did not require them to be constant or independent.

The proof is conditional. If a disturbance exceeds 0.4, if the command arrives late, or if the
temperature is not known as assumed, a hypothesis has changed. The proof remains
mathematically valid while its applicability to that modified situation needs new work. Keeping
hypotheses beside the step where they are used makes that limitation understandable rather than
an unexplained disclaimer at the end.


A limit does not automatically give a stopping time

A controller may run a numerical calculation before issuing its command. Let ek be a nonnegative
                                                                                      ​




temperature prediction error bound after iteration k . An error recurrence describes how an earlier
error is reduced and how new approximation error is introduced. The geometric factor explains the

   Using w = 0 in the chamber or dropping 0.02 from the error recurrence makes the arithmetic


VI-A Continuous-time Example


To illustrate the influence of the penalty on the value function, we solve the penalized problem analytically on a simple continuous-time example.
We consider the following piecewise-affine system:




x˙​(t)={−LTf,if​x∈(−L,0),u⁡(x⁡(t)),if​x∈[0,L),0,otherwise,\dot{x}(t)=\begin{cases}-\frac{L}{{T_{f}}},&\text{if}~x\in(-L,0),\\
u(x(t)),&\text{if}~x\in[0,L),\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(25)

with state space 𝒳=[−L,L]\mathcal{X}=[-L,L] and finite control space 𝒰={−v,0,v}\mathcal{U}=\{-v,0,v\}, where LL and vv are positive constants.
We define failure with 𝒳F={−L}{\mathcal{X}_{F}}=\{-L\}; the trajectory immediately stops when it enters 𝒳F{\mathcal{X}_{F}}.
This results in the viability kernel 𝒳V=[0,L]{\mathcal{X}_{V}}=[0,L] and unviability kernel 𝒳U=(−L,0){\mathcal{X}_{U}}=(-L,0).
It is straightforward to verify that Assumption 1 holds with constant Tf{T_{f}}.
Intuitively, this model can be thought of as a robot driving on a shelf of size [0,L][0,L]; the robot can move forward or backward at constant speed ±v\pm v.
If, however, the robot crosses the ledge of the shelf at x=0x=0, it falls at constant speed L/Tf{L}/{{T_{f}}} until it reaches the ground at x=−Lx=-L and breaks.


We use the following reward function:




r⁡(x)={−1,if​x∈[0,L),1,if​x∈(−L,0),0,if​x∈{−L,L}.r(x)=\begin{cases}-1,&\text{if}~x\in[0,L),\\
1,&\text{if}~x\in(-L,0),\\
0,&\text{if}~x\in\{-L,L\}.\end{cases}

(26)

This reward leads the agent to failure from some viable states.
We therefore also penalize failure with the penalty pp; the total reward is r−p⋅δ𝒳Fr-p\cdot\delta_{{\mathcal{X}_{F}}}.
We illustrate with this example how a choice of p>p⋆p>p^{\star} yields an SVF and how p⋆p^{\star}, τ\tau, and Tf{T_{f}} interact.


VI-A1 Scaling the Penalty


The optimal control consists in either going to and remaining at x=Lx=L, or falling from the shelf.
From this observation we deduce the value function for x∈𝒳Vx\in{\mathcal{X}_{V}}:




Vp​(x)=max{τ[ex−Lv⋅τ−1],τ⁡[(2−e−Tfτ)⋅e−xv⋅τ−1]−p⋅e−Tf+xvτ}.\begin{split}V_{p}(x)=\max~&\left\{\tau\left[\vphantom{e^{-\frac{{T_{f}}}{\tau}}}e^{\frac{x-L}{v\cdot\tau}}-1\right],\right.\\
&\left.~~\tau\left[\left(2-e^{-\frac{{T_{f}}}{\tau}}\right)\cdot e^{-\frac{x}{v\cdot\tau}}-1\right]\right.\\
&\left.\qquad-p\cdot e^{-\frac{{T_{f}}+\frac{x}{v}}{\tau}}\right\}.\end{split}

(27)

In contrast, in 𝒳U{\mathcal{X}_{U}}, the trajectory goes to failure at speed LTf\frac{L}{{T_{f}}}; we get the following expression for x∈𝒳Ux\in{\mathcal{X}_{U}}:




Vp​(x)=τ⁡[1−e−Tfτ​(1+xL)]−p⋅e−Tfτ​(1+xL).V_{p}(x)=\tau\left[1-e^{-\frac{{T_{f}}}{\tau}\left(1+\frac{x}{L}\right)}\right]-p\cdot e^{-\frac{{T_{f}}}{\tau}\left(1+\frac{x}{L}\right)}.

(28)

The agent thus remains in 𝒳V{\mathcal{X}_{V}} for all time if, and only if, the max\max in (27) is equal to its first argument in x=0x=0.
This yields the minimum value for pp for an SVF:




p>τ⁡[2−e−Lv⋅τ]⋅eTfτ−τ​=˙​p⋆.p>\tau\left[2-e^{-\frac{L}{v\cdot\tau}}\right]\cdot e^{\frac{{T_{f}}}{\tau}}-\tau~\dot{=}~p^{\star}.

(29)

The RHS is equal to p⋆p^{\star} as defined in (18a); we recover Theorem 2 without directly using it.


As pp passes p⋆p^{\star}, values in 𝒳U{\mathcal{X}_{U}} keep decreasing and the difference between the thresholds αinf\alpha_{\mathrm{inf}} and αsup\alpha_{\mathrm{sup}} increases (Fig. 2, top).
The viability kernel can easily be recovered via thresholding thanks to the discontinuity in x=0x=0.
Nevertheless, the value α=0\alpha=0 is not a suitable threshold; this is due to the negative values of the reward in 𝒳V{\mathcal{X}_{V}}.
We further illustrate this in Section VI-E.



VI-A2 Discount Rate, Time-to-Failure, and Penalties


While Equation (18) is in general only an upper-bound, it is tight on this example; p⋆p^{\star} is the lowest penalty such that VpV_{p} is safe for p>p⋆p>p^{\star}.
This emphasizes the role of the discount rate τ\tau and time-to-failure Tf{T_{f}} on the minimum penalty (Fig. 2, middle and bottom).
A large Tf{T_{f}} can lead to a large penalty due to the exponential dependency p⋆∝eTf/τp^{\star}\propto e^{{{T_{f}}}/{\tau}}.
A higher discount rate may help mitigate that problem; it more easily propagates the penalty signal up a failed trajectory.
However, the discount rate is also a crucial parameter determining the safe, optimal behavior.
In fact, if the reward function can be negative inside 𝒳V{\mathcal{X}_{V}}, a large τ\tau can actually lower inf𝒳VV\inf_{\mathcal{X}_{V}}V (the RHS of (11)), increase p⋆p^{\star}, and thus encourage failure.


A suitable choice of τ\tau needs to consider both of these independent criteria, even if the time-to-failure Tf{T_{f}} is the main driver of this choice.
A long Tf{T_{f}} essentially indicates that there exist controllers that can delay an inevitable failure.
While it may seem unintuitive that the existence of such controllers increases the relative gravity of failure, it adds a new interpretation to the benefit of “fail fast”.
We may conclude that if there are two causally related unviable states, it is advantageous to penalize the one that occurs first.

From the mathematics to a real decision

Learning objectives

  • Build a safe region from bounded-noise observations without treating the GP as the safety authority.
  • Propagate uncertainty in the implemented experiment through a Lipschitz certificate.
  • Convert a finite-horizon Gaussian noise assumption into an explicitly probabilistic noise envelope.

A commissioning decision

Continue tuning the enclosure's dimensionless gain, but now separate the search model from the safety mechanism. The unknown headroom $f(a)$ is fixed, has units degrees Celsius, and is assumed to satisfy $|f(a)-f(b)|\le0.8|a-b|$. A reading is $y_i=f(a_i)+\epsilon_i$. For the first part, assume the sensor has a deterministic error bound $|\epsilon_i|\le E=0.15$ degrees for every experiment. This is stronger than knowing its standard deviation.

A baseline gain $a_0=0$ is known safe independently. Its reading is $y_0=0.95$ degrees. A later reading $y_1=0.75$ is collected at gain $a_1=0.8$. The second experiment is legitimate because it will be certified from the first reading. The domain for this example is the whole real line of gains; any actual hardware limits would require an additional intersection.

A GP may rank candidates for performance, including candidates where it predicts unusually large thermal headroom. The safety gate instead uses the reading-minus-error lower bound and the known Lipschitz constant. Assume the measurement bound includes all sensor error relevant to the standardized experiment and that the headroom definition includes the actual trajectory maximum.

Worked decision, with its limits

Turn one observation into a cone. At the baseline, $f(0)\ge y_0-E=0.80$ degrees. At a candidate gain $a$, Lipschitz continuity therefore gives

$$f(a)\ge0.80-0.8|a|.$$

The right side is nonnegative for $|a|\le1$, so the first certified interval is $[-1,1]$. In particular, gain 0.8 may be measured without relying on its GP prediction. This is the induction step: only a point already certified by earlier evidence supplies the next reading.

Add the second certificate. The second reading gives $f(0.8)\ge0.75-0.15=0.60$ degrees. Its radius is $0.60/0.8=0.75$, yielding $[0.05,1.55]$. The union of the two intervals is $[-1,1.55]$. A point needs one valid witness; requiring every observation to certify it would unnecessarily take an intersection instead.

Check two actual proposals. Gain 1.4 receives lower bound $0.60-0.8|1.4-0.8|=0.12$ degrees from the second reading and is admissible. Gain 1.7 receives $0.60-0.8(0.9)=-0.12$; the baseline gives an even lower bound. It remains uncertified even if the GP predicts excellent performance there.

Interpret the authority of the surrogate. An inaccurate GP can cause inefficient choices inside $[-1,1.55]$ without changing this safety proof. It cannot enlarge that set. The deterministic conclusion still rests on exact assumptions about $L=0.8$, $E=0.15$, a fixed function, and experiment execution. A drifting thermal load or a wrong Lipschitz constant changes the physical problem rather than merely making the model's predictions less accurate.

The cone radius is a budget: observed slack pays for sensor uncertainty and parameter displacement. Improving acquisition does not recover slack already spent by these terms. Better sensor calibration or a justified smaller Lipschitz bound may enlarge the region, but only after new evidence supports those replacements.

A tempting wrong approach

Common mistake — A noise scale is a hard bound

A Gaussian sensor with standard deviation 0.15 degrees does not satisfy $|\epsilon_i|\le0.15$ deterministically. Inserting that number into the preceding cone and calling the result always safe drops the tail events. Likewise, observing small errors in a short calibration run is not a universal error bound. The finite-horizon alternative in B2 makes the probability and intended number of experiments explicit.

Transfer the argument

Exercise 5.B1 — Medium: Certify the gain the actuator might implement

The command $a$ is realized somewhere in $[a-0.1,a+0.1]$. Using the certified union $[-1,1.55]$, derive the allowable command interval. Does command 1.5 pass?

Review: Bounded-noise safety cones.

Show hint

Require containment of the entire realization interval, including both endpoints.

Show worked solution

Certify the gain actually executed. The previous evidence certifies every gain in $S=[-1,1.55]$. A command $a$ may be realized as $a+\varepsilon$ with $|\varepsilon|\le0.1$, so all possible implementations fill $[a-0.1,a+0.1]$. The command passes only if this entire interval lies in $S$.

Check both extreme realizations. Because both sets are intervals, containment is equivalent to requiring the smallest realization to be no smaller than the certified lower endpoint and the largest to be no larger than its upper endpoint:

$$\begin{aligned} a-0.1\ge-1&\iff a\ge-1+0.1=-0.9,\\ a+0.1\le1.55&\iff a\le1.55-0.1=1.45. \end{aligned}$$

The allowable command interval is therefore $[-0.9,1.45]$.

Test command 1.5. Its possible realizations are $[1.4,1.6]$. The upper realization 1.6 is outside the certified union, since $1.6\gt1.55$, so command 1.5 is not robustly certified. The command's nominal value being inside $S$ does not cover that implementation error. An excluded gain need not actually be unsafe; it has no guarantee from the available cones.

At command 1.45 the largest realized gain is exactly 1.55, where the second cone's lower headroom is $0.60-0.8|1.55-0.8|=0.60-0.8(0.75)=0$. This is allowed by the non-strict requirement $f\ge0$. The lower endpoint command $-0.9$ likewise permits realization $-1$, at the first cone's zero-margin boundary. To certify strictly positive headroom for every realization using these cones, choose commands strictly inside $(-0.9,1.45)$. The noise, Lipschitz and experiment-execution assumptions remain part of the certificate.

Exercise 5.B2 — Hard: Replace deterministic noise by a finite-run event

Instead assume each error has conditional Gaussian distribution with zero mean and standard deviation 0.15 degrees, even when gains are chosen adaptively. Plan at most 100 experiments and allow noise-envelope failure probability 0.01. Using $\mathbb P(|\epsilon|\gt E)\le2\exp[-E^2/(2(0.15)^2)]$, find a sufficient common $E$ and the radius certified by the first reading 0.95.

Review: Noise assumptions in the safety proof.

Show hint

Make each tail at most $0.01/100$, then sum the tail probabilities. Independence is unnecessary for this union bound.

Show worked solution

Keep the assumption valid after adaptive choices. Let $\mathcal F_{i-1}$ be the information used to choose experiment $i$. The question assumes that, conditional on that information, its sensor error is still Gaussian with mean zero and standard deviation $0.15$ degrees. Thus the supplied tail bound gives

$$\Pr(|\epsilon_i|\gt E\mid\mathcal F_{i-1}) \le2\exp\left(-\frac{E^2}{2(0.15)^2}\right).$$

Averaging conditional probabilities over the histories gives the unconditional probability. Since the right-hand side is the same constant for every history,

$$\Pr(|\epsilon_i|\gt E) =\mathbb E\big[\Pr(|\epsilon_i|\gt E\mid\mathcal F_{i-1})\big] \le2\exp\left(-\frac{E^2}{2(0.15)^2}\right).$$

This is why adaptive gain selection does not invalidate the stated bound; a merely unconditional Gaussian description would not automatically supply this conditional premise.

Allocate the envelope-failure budget across the whole run. A union bound over at most 100 experiments yields

$$\Pr\big(\text{some }|\epsilon_i|\gt E\big) \le\sum_{i=1}^{100}\Pr(|\epsilon_i|\gt E) \le200\exp\left(-\frac{E^2}{2(0.15)^2}\right).$$

No independence between these failure events is needed. To make their combined probability at most 0.01, solve

$$\begin{aligned} 200\exp\left(-\frac{E^2}{2(0.15)^2}\right)&\le0.01\\ \exp\left(-\frac{E^2}{2(0.15)^2}\right)&\le\frac{0.01}{200}=\frac1{20000}\\ -\frac{E^2}{2(0.15)^2}&\le-\ln(20000)\\ E^2&\ge2(0.15)^2\ln(20000). \end{aligned}$$

The logarithm is increasing; multiplying the resulting inequality by a negative number reverses its direction. An envelope radius is nonnegative, so the smallest sufficient choice from this tail bound is

$$E=0.15\sqrt{2\ln(20000)}\approx0.667575\ \text{degrees}.$$

Convert the first observation into a safe cone on that envelope event. When every error is within $E$, the reading $y_0=0.95$ implies $f(0)\ge0.95-E\approx0.282425$ degrees. With Lipschitz constant $L=0.8$ and threshold zero,

$$f(a)\ge0.95-E-0.8|a|\ge0 \quad\text{if}\quad |a|\le r:=\frac{0.95-E}{0.8}\approx0.353031.$$

The domain in this lab is the entire real line, so the first cone certifies $[-r,r]$. The numerator is positive here; a negative numerator would provide no certificate from this observation, though the independently validated seed would remain known safe.

On the simultaneous envelope event, the deterministic cone/previously-safe-query induction applies throughout the planned run. Its failure probability is at most 0.01, hence the safe-run guarantee is at least 0.99 under the stated seed, fixed-function, Lipschitz and execution assumptions. The smaller radius than the original deterministic radius 1 pays for Gaussian tails over 100 uses. This finite-horizon bound cannot be extended beyond 100 experiments without another justified probability budget or an all-time bound; Gaussian standard deviation alone is never a deterministic error envelope.

Synthesis and bridge

A safe set can be independent of the GP while still depending strongly on physical regularity and noise assumptions. That separation makes it easier to diagnose a failed prediction: the surrogate's poor ranking and the gate's invalid assumptions are different failure modes. Recording which observation certifies a candidate also makes the decision reviewable.

Even a valid cone may leave a desirable parameter region unreachable. The next chapter introduces state-aware backups: the robot may safely test a policy whose parameters lack a local certificate if its trajectory can be monitored and a validated controller can recover from every state reached before switching.

1. Forward vs. Inverse: Which Direction Is Hard?

In a forward problem you are given the causes — loads, material parameters, geometry, boundary conditions — and you compute the effects: displacements, strains, sensor readings. Module 2 was entirely forward. The inverse problem reverses the inference: you observe noisy effects and must estimate the causes that produced them.

Core Problem
Given noisy measurements of a mechanical system's response, estimate the causes — loads, material parameters, boundary data, or the deformed state — when the forward map has already destroyed part of the information you need. Formally, with forward operator $G$, model parameters $\mathbf{m}$, and noise $\mathbf{e}$: $$\mathbf{d} = G(\mathbf{m}) + \mathbf{e}, \qquad\qquad \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{e} \quad \text{(discrete linear case)}$$

The asymmetry between the two directions is physical, not computational. Forward operators in continuum mechanics are smoothing: elliptic boundary-value problems damp high-spatial-frequency components of the input. By Saint-Venant-type reasoning, two statically equivalent but locally different load distributions produce nearly identical fields a short distance away. The forward map compresses distinct causes into nearly indistinguishable effects.

Concretely, in vision-based tactile sensing: a contact pressure profile on a soft skin's surface produces marker displacements measured at depth $h$ below the surface. Pressure detail finer than roughly the embedding depth is blurred away, so two distinct contact profiles map to nearly indistinguishable camera data. Inverting means un-smoothing — dividing by numbers close to zero — which amplifies measurement noise without bound.

The same structure appears everywhere:

  • Image deblurring — blur (forward) is easy and stable; deblurring is unstable.
  • Hearing a drum — computing the sound of a known drum is forward; inferring the drum's shape from its sound is inverse.
  • Gravimetric prospecting — computing surface gravity from a known density distribution is forward; mapping subsurface density from surface gravity is inverse.
The forward map $G$ compresses well-separated parameters into nearly coincident data. Inversion must undo the compression: the preimage of the noise ball is an elongated sliver of mutually consistent parameters.

Three framings recur throughout this guide, in increasing order of difficulty of the unknown:

FramingUnknownExample
Parameter identificationA finite vector of numbersYoung's modulus, a handful of hyperelastic constants
Field / distributed reconstructionA function — discretizes to many unknowns; the hard caseA contact pressure map, a spatial modulus field
State reconstructionThe current state of a known systemiFEM's displacement-field-from-strain problem (Module 4)
Key insight — the thesis of this module
The forward map loses information. The data alone cannot give it back. Every "solution" of an ill-posed inverse problem is data plus injected prior assumptions, and honest methods make the injection explicit. Regularization (Sections 7–9) is not a numerical trick — it is a modeling decision.

2. The Observation Model: One Equation, Four Families

Before any machinery, fix the vocabulary that the whole guide runs on. Every inverse method in Modules 4–12 is an instance of a single observation model, and every method is fully characterized by how it answers four questions: what is measured, what is unknown, what is assumed known, and what is unobservable.

The Observation Model (guide-wide spine)
$$\mathbf{y} = \mathcal{H}(\mathbf{u},\, \mathbf{p},\, \mathbf{f},\, \mathbf{X}_0) + \mathbf{b} + \boldsymbol{\eta}$$
$\mathbf{y}$measurements (strains, marker positions, forces, images)
$\mathcal{H}$observation operator: the physics (Module 2's forward FEM) composed with the sensor model
$\mathbf{u}$state — the displacement/deformation field
$\mathbf{p}$material parameters or parameter fields
$\mathbf{f}$loads and boundary tractions
$\mathbf{X}_0$reference (unloaded) configuration
$\mathbf{b}$systematic bias: model error, miscalibration, drift
$\boldsymbol{\eta}$random measurement noise

Choosing which argument of $\mathcal{H}$ is unknown — and assuming the rest — generates four distinct inverse families. They share the machinery of this module, but they are separate problems with different conditioning, different priors, and different literatures; none is a variant of another:

UnknownFamilyWhere in this guide
$\mathbf{u}$Shape sensing / state reconstruction (iFEM)Modules 4–6
$\mathbf{p}$Material identification (FEMU, VFM, elastography)Module 7
$\mathbf{f}$Load and contact-force inversionModule 8
$\mathbf{X}_0$Reference-configuration recoveryModule 9

A notation collision, flagged once: in the spine equation $\mathbf{b}$ denotes systematic bias; in this module's linear algebra, following the numerical-analysis convention, $\mathbf{b}$ is the data vector in $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$. Context disambiguates — the spine's bias term will not reappear until the validation discussion in Section 13, where structured residuals reveal it.

Rank, Nullspace, and What "Unobservable" Means

Linearize $\mathcal{H}$ about an operating point and collect the derivatives into a matrix $\mathbf{A}$ (the sensitivity or observation matrix). Two subspaces of the unknown space now decide everything:

  • rank$(\mathbf{A})$ counts the independently observable directions — the number of distinct pieces of information your sensor set can deliver, at most $\min(m,n)$.
  • The nullspace, $\{\mathbf{v} : \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\}$, contains the unknown components your sensors cannot see at all. Adding any nullspace vector to a solution changes nothing in the predicted data.

Mechanics supplies canonical nullspaces: strain gauges are blind to rigid-body motion (rigid translation and rotation produce zero strain), and boundary sensors cannot distinguish interior load distributions that are statically equivalent. Two terms of art, used consistently from here on: observability asks whether a state or field ($\mathbf{u}$, $\mathbf{X}_0$-type unknowns) is determined by the measurements; identifiability asks the same for parameters ($\mathbf{p}$-type unknowns). For linear problems both reduce to the nullspace of $\mathbf{A}$; for nonlinear parameter dependence, the rank of the Jacobian governs identifiability only locally.

Between "observable" and "unobservable" lies the practically important middle ground: directions that $\mathbf{A}$ maps to tiny but nonzero outputs. They are formally observable and practically lost in noise. Quantifying that middle ground is exactly what the SVD does in Section 5.

Key insight
A nullspace is a property of the sensor set, not of the algorithm. No regularizer can restore an unobservable component — it can only fill it in from the prior. If an unobservable component matters, the fix is to change the experiment (add sensors, move them, measure a different quantity), not to change the solver.

3. Hadamard Well-Posedness and Why Elasticity Inversion Breaks It

Hadamard (1923) called a problem well-posed if it passes three tests. A problem failing any one of them is ill-posed.

Definition — Hadamard well-posedness
  1. Existence: a solution exists for all admissible data.
  2. Uniqueness: the solution is unique.
  3. Stability: the solution depends continuously on the data.

Each condition fails in a concrete, mechanical way:

Existence. Noisy data $\mathbf{b}$ generally lies outside the range of $\mathbf{A}$ in an overdetermined system — no $\mathbf{x}$ satisfies $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ exactly. Least squares (Section 4) repairs this by asking for the closest achievable fit instead.

Uniqueness. A nullspace: strain gauges cannot see rigid-body motion; boundary sensors cannot distinguish statically equivalent interior load distributions (Section 2).

Stability. The canonical example is differentiation of noisy data. Perturb $g(x)$ by a tiny high-frequency wiggle and watch the derivative:

$$g_\varepsilon(x) = g(x) + \varepsilon\sin(kx) \;\Longrightarrow\; g_\varepsilon'(x) = g'(x) + \varepsilon k\cos(kx)$$

As $k$ grows, the data perturbation stays at size $\varepsilon$ while the answer perturbation $\varepsilon k$ blows up without bound. The same mechanism drives the backward heat equation, where recovering the initial state amplifies mode $k$ by $e^{k^2 T}$.

The Structural Reason: Compact Operators

The instability is not bad luck; it is built into the physics. When the forward map is a compact operator between infinite-dimensional spaces — the situation for a first-kind Fredholm integral equation, which is the continuous model of deconvolution and of load-to-strain maps —

$$\int_0^1 k(s,t)\,f(t)\,dt = g(s) \qquad \text{(Fredholm equation of the first kind)}$$

a bounded inverse cannot exist, so condition (3) fails structurally, not accidentally (Engl, Hanke & Neubauer 1996). Discretization does not remove the pathology; it converts the unbounded inverse into a finite matrix whose condition number grows without bound as the mesh refines.

In elasticity specifically (Bonnet & Constantinescu 2005): the Cauchy problem — reconstructing tractions and displacements on an inaccessible boundary from overdetermined data on an accessible one — is severely ill-posed, while interior parameter-field identification from full-field data is more mildly ill-posed. The severity spectrum matters practically and returns in Section 12.

History then reversed itself: Hadamard considered ill-posed problems physically meaningless; Tikhonov's school (Tikhonov & Arsenin 1977) showed they are meaningful once prior information restores stability. The repair kit maps one-to-one onto the failures:

Failed conditionRepairWhere
ExistenceLeast squares: minimize the residual instead of solving exactlySection 4
UniquenessPrior/penalty structure selects one solution from the consistent setSections 7, 11
StabilityRegularization filters the noise-amplifying componentsSections 7–9

4. Linear Least Squares and the Normal Equations

The workhorse setting: $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with $m \ge n$ and full column rank. Since noisy $\mathbf{b}$ is not in the range of $\mathbf{A}$, minimize the squared residual instead. Setting the gradient of the quadratic $\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_2^2$ to zero gives the normal equations:

$$\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{x}} \|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A}\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}$$

Geometric reading: $\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{b}$ onto range$(\mathbf{A})$; the residual is orthogonal to every column of $\mathbf{A}$. The solution $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{A}^\dagger\mathbf{b}$ defines the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse for full-rank $\mathbf{A}$.

Conditioning: How Many Digits Survive?

The spectral condition number measures noise amplification through a linear solve (singular values $\sigma_i$ formally defined in Section 5):

$$\kappa_2(\mathbf{A}) = \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_n}, \qquad \kappa_2(\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A}) = \kappa_2(\mathbf{A})^2$$

Rule of thumb: a linear solve loses about $\log_{10}\kappa$ decimal digits; double precision holds about 16. The precise perturbation facts, needed later: for consistent systems the relative error in $\mathbf{x}$ is bounded by $\kappa(\mathbf{A})$ times the relative error in $\mathbf{b}$; for least-squares problems with a large residual, an additional $\kappa(\mathbf{A})^2$ term enters even for backward-stable algorithms. So "least squares amplifies errors by $\kappa$" is only true in the small-residual regime, and only for the right algorithm — always state which algorithm and which regime.

Why it matters — never form $\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A}$
Forming the normal equations squares the condition number: $\kappa_2(\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A}) = \kappa_2(\mathbf{A})^2$. With $\kappa(\mathbf{A}) = 10^8$ — typical for a mildly ill-posed identification matrix — $\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A}$ is numerically singular in double precision even though $\mathbf{A}$ itself still carries 8 digits of information. Remedy: QR factorization ($\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}$, solve $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{Q}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}$) or the SVD, which work at $\kappa(\mathbf{A})$, not $\kappa(\mathbf{A})^2$. The same warning applies verbatim to $\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{J}$ in Gauss–Newton (Section 10).

Ill-conditioning hides in innocuous-looking matrices. The $n\times n$ Hilbert matrix — a classic mildly ill-posed Gram matrix with clean analytic entries — has $\kappa(\mathbf{H}_5) \approx 4.8\times10^5$ and $\kappa(\mathbf{H}_{10}) \approx 1.6\times10^{13}$: at $n=10$ it has already exhausted double precision.

Foreshadowing the rest of the module: for well-conditioned $\mathbf{A}$, least squares is the end of the story and no regularization is needed. Regularization earns its place only when $\kappa$ is large and the singular values decay gradually — which is Section 5's diagnosis.

Weighted Least Squares, Whitening, and Correlated Noise

Ordinary least squares implicitly assumes every measurement deserves equal trust. When the noise has covariance $\mathbf{C} = \operatorname{cov}(\mathbf{e})$, the statistically correct functional weights the residual by the inverse covariance (the Gaussian likelihood of Section 11 makes this exact; Kaipio & Somersalo 2005):

$$\min_{\mathbf{x}} \;(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})^\mathsf{T}\,\mathbf{C}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})$$

Equivalently, whiten: multiply both $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ by $\mathbf{C}^{-1/2}$ and solve an ordinary least-squares problem whose noise is white with unit variance. Dividing each row by its sensor channel's noise standard deviation is the diagonal special case. After whitening — and only after — the clean statements of Sections 5–9 hold: the noise floor is flat, the discrepancy principle has a known target, GCV's assumptions are met.

Why it matters — weights are a claim about your noise
Weighted least squares delivers statistical precision only if the covariance model is right. A wrong $\mathbf{C}$ does not merely lose efficiency — it systematically distorts the solution toward fitting the channels you overtrust. Camera-derived measurements that pass through spatial post-processing (interpolation, smoothing, marker tracking) carry spatially correlated noise: treating it as white is itself a modeling error, and it resurfaces as GCV's classic failure mode in Section 9.

5. The SVD: Anatomy of an Ill-Conditioned Problem

The singular value decomposition is the diagnostic instrument for everything in this module. It rotates the problem into a basis where the forward map is a plain diagonal scaling:

$$\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\mathsf{T} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sigma_i\,\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\mathsf{T}$$

with orthonormal left/right singular vectors $\mathbf{u}_i$, $\mathbf{v}_i$ and $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_n \ge 0$. In this basis the least-squares solution is an explicit expansion, and splitting the data $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{exact}} + \mathbf{e}$ exposes exactly where inversion goes wrong:

$$\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{LS}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}}{\sigma_i}\,\mathbf{v}_i = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{exact}}}{\sigma_i}\,\mathbf{v}_i}_{\text{signal}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{e}}{\sigma_i}\,\mathbf{v}_i}_{\text{amplified noise}}$$

Two facts drive everything that follows:

Fact 1 — white noise is flat in the singular basis. The exact coefficients $\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{exact}}$ decay with $i$ (Section 6), but for white noise of standard deviation $s$, every $|\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{e}| \approx s$ regardless of $i$ — an orthogonal rotation preserves white noise. So component $i$ of the naive solution carries a noise term of size $s/\sigma_i$: and once $\sigma_i$ is small that term is enormous. Be careful about the exact criterion, though: what decides whether component $i$ is recoverable is not $\sigma_i$ against $s$, but the exact data coefficient $|\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{exact}}|$ against $s$. Once the signal in a direction has sunk below the noise, inverting it returns amplified noise no matter how the singular value compares.

Fact 2 — small-$\sigma$ singular vectors are oscillatory. In discrete ill-posed problems, singular vectors associated with small $\sigma_i$ have increasingly many sign changes — the matrix version of "the forward map kills high frequencies". Hence the amplified garbage is high-frequency, which is why an unregularized solution looks like noise rather than like a slightly-wrong answer.

A Taxonomy of Singular Spectra

Plot $\sigma_i$ against $i$ on a semilog axis and the problem type reads off directly (Hansen 1998; Hansen 2010):

Three singular spectra, three regimes. Well-conditioned: ordinary least squares suffices. Rank-deficient: a gap defines the numerical rank $r$; truncate there. Discrete ill-posed: gradual decay with no gap — no natural rank exists, and the truncation point becomes a free parameter you must choose.
SpectrumSignatureDiagnosis
Well-conditioned$\sigma_i$ roughly flatOrdinary least squares; stop, no regularization
Rank-deficientCluster of large $\sigma_i$, then a sharp gap down to machine-precision levelNumerical rank is well defined; TSVD at the gap (Section 8)
Discrete ill-posedGradual decay to zero with no gap — algebraic $\sigma_i \sim i^{-p}$ (mildly ill-posed) or exponential (severely ill-posed)No meaningful rank exists; the truncation/regularization level is a free parameter (Sections 7–9)

For calibration of expectations: deconvolution with a Gaussian kernel is severely ill-posed — its spectrum decays like a Gaussian in frequency — while many elasticity identification operators are mildly to moderately ill-posed.

Key insight — refinement makes it worse
The ill-conditioning is inherited from the continuous compact operator (Section 3), so refining the discretization makes $\kappa$ worse, not better — "add more elements" is not a fix. Conversely, a coarse mesh is itself a crude implicit regularizer: if you then add an explicit penalty on top, know that you are regularizing twice.

6. The Discrete Picard Condition

Small singular values alone do not doom a problem — what matters is how fast the data's SVD coefficients fall relative to the singular values. That comparison is the discrete Picard condition, and its plot is the single most informative diagnostic you can produce before attempting any inversion.

Definition — Discrete Picard Condition (Hansen 1990)
A discrete ill-posed problem has a meaningful, bounded-norm underlying solution only if the SVD coefficients of the exact data decay to zero faster on average than the singular values: $$\text{DPC:}\quad |\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{exact}}| \;\text{decays faster than}\; \sigma_i \;\text{(on average)}$$ If they decay slower, even noise-free inversion produces a solution whose norm diverges under mesh refinement — the discrete shadow of the classical Picard condition for first-kind Fredholm equations.

Reading a Picard Plot

On a semilog axis versus index $i$, plot three things: $\sigma_i$, the data coefficients $|\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}|$, and their ratio $|\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}|/\sigma_i$. With real (noisy) data the coefficients track the decaying exact coefficients only until they hit the noise floor at the per-sample noise standard deviation $s$, where they level off into a horizontal band — because $|\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{e}| \approx s$ for all $i$ (Section 5):

$$|\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}| \approx \max\left\{\,|\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{exact}}|,\; s\,\right\}, \qquad s = \text{noise std. dev.}$$
Anatomy of a Picard plot. The crossing index $i^*$ — where the data coefficients $|\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}|$ flatten onto the noise plateau — is the information content of the experiment: more unknowns do not raise it; better sensors do. ($\sigma_i$ reaching that same level, further right, is a different and later event.)

Define the crossing index $i^*$: the index at which the measured coefficients stop tracking the exact ones and flatten onto the noise plateau — equivalently, where $|\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{exact}}|$ sinks to $s$. Components $i < i^*$ carry recoverable signal; beyond it the coefficients are noise, so the ratio grows like $s/\sigma_i$ and inverting those directions amplifies noise. Note what $i^*$ is not: the index where $\sigma_i$ itself drops below $s$. That crossing is a different (and later) event — in the figure above the coefficients flatten at $i^* \approx 16$ while $\sigma_i$ only reaches the noise level near $i = 25$ — and using it would overstate the information you actually hold. Recoverability is decided by the data coefficients, not by the singular values alone. $i^*$ is the honest measure of the information content of the experiment — often brutally small. In a 64-point Gaussian deconvolution at 1% noise, typically only on the order of 10 components are recoverable, regardless of how many unknowns were discretized.

The experiment-design reading: adding sensors, moving them closer to the source, or reducing noise raises $i^*$. Adding unknowns does not.

Key insight — the plateau is not a violation
The leveling-off of $|\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}|$ does not mean the discrete Picard condition is violated. The DPC concerns the exact data; the plateau is the visible signature of the noise and is expected in every real dataset. Reading the plateau as a broken Picard condition is a classic misreading — what the plateau actually tells you is where the recoverable components end.

This diagnosis motivates both regularization families: TSVD keeps components $i \le k \approx i^*$ (Section 8); Tikhonov rolls components off smoothly around $\sigma_i \approx \lambda$ (Section 7).

7. Tikhonov Regularization

Tikhonov's repair (Tikhonov & Arsenin 1977): stop asking for the best data fit, and minimize a penalized functional that trades fidelity against a prior notion of "reasonable":

$$\mathbf{x}_\lambda = \arg\min_{\mathbf{x}}\left\{ \|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 + \lambda^2\|\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)\|_2^2 \right\}$$

The seminorm matrix $\mathbf{L}$ and reference $\mathbf{x}_0$ encode the prior. Standard form: $\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{I}$, $\mathbf{x}_0=\mathbf{0}$ — penalize magnitude. Common general forms: $\mathbf{L}$ = discrete first-derivative (bidiagonal) or Laplacian (tridiagonal) operator — penalize roughness, appropriate when the unknown field (e.g. a contact pressure distribution under a smooth indenter) is expected smooth. Setting the gradient to zero gives the regularized normal equations, uniquely solvable — and, for $\lambda$ not too small, far better conditioned than $\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A}$ alone — provided $\mathbf{L}$ actually covers what $\mathbf{A}$ cannot see, i.e. $\ker\mathbf{A}\cap\ker\mathbf{L}=\{\mathbf{0}\}$. That condition is automatic for $\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{I}$ but not for derivative penalties: a first-difference $\mathbf{L}$ has constants in its nullspace and a Laplacian has constants and linears, so if $\mathbf{A}$ is also blind to those modes, no $\lambda$ will pin them down.

$$\left(\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A} + \lambda^2\mathbf{L}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{L}\right)\mathbf{x}_\lambda = \mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b} + \lambda^2\mathbf{L}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{L}\,\mathbf{x}_0$$

Filter Factors: Tikhonov in the Singular Basis

For $\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{I}$ the SVD diagonalizes everything, and the Tikhonov solution is a filtered version of the least-squares expansion:

$$\mathbf{x}_\lambda = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i\,\frac{\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}}{\sigma_i}\,\mathbf{v}_i, \qquad f_i = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_i^2+\lambda^2} \quad (\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{I})$$

The filter factors interpolate smoothly between keep and kill: $f_i \approx 1$ for $\sigma_i \gg \lambda$ (component kept), $f_i \approx \sigma_i^2/\lambda^2 \to 0$ for $\sigma_i \ll \lambda$ (component suppressed). Tikhonov is a low-pass filter in the singular basis, with $\lambda$ the cutoff singular value.

The Bias–Noise Trade-Off Is a U-Shaped Curve

Substituting $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{exact}} + \mathbf{e}$ into the filtered expansion splits the error into two opposing terms:

$$\mathbf{x}_\lambda - \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{exact}} = \underbrace{-\sum_{i=1}^{n} (1-f_i)\,(\mathbf{v}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{exact}})\,\mathbf{v}_i}_{\text{regularization error (bias): grows with }\lambda} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i\,\frac{\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{e}}{\sigma_i}\,\mathbf{v}_i}_{\text{perturbation error (noise): shrinks with }\lambda}$$
Full derivation: the bias–noise split

Write the exact data as $\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{exact}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{exact}}$. Then $\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{exact}} = \mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{exact}} = \sigma_i\,\mathbf{v}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{exact}}$, because $\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A} = \sigma_i \mathbf{v}_i^\mathsf{T}$. Substituting $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{exact}} + \mathbf{e}$ into the filtered expansion:

$$\mathbf{x}_\lambda = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i\,(\mathbf{v}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{exact}})\,\mathbf{v}_i + \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i\,\frac{\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{e}}{\sigma_i}\,\mathbf{v}_i$$

Subtract $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{exact}} = \sum_i (\mathbf{v}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{exact}})\,\mathbf{v}_i$ and the two terms above appear. As $\lambda$ grows, every $f_i$ falls: the bias term (the signal components you filtered away) grows, and the noise term (the amplified noise you let through) shrinks. The total error is U-shaped in $\lambda$, and the entire parameter-choice problem of Section 9 is locating the bottom of the U without knowing $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{exact}}$.

Numerical Practice

Never form $\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A}$ (Section 4). Solve the augmented least-squares system by QR instead — it is an ordinary least-squares problem with the penalty rows stacked beneath the data rows:

$$\min_{\mathbf{x}}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}\\ \lambda\mathbf{L}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x} - \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{b}\\ \lambda\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x}_0\end{bmatrix}\right\|_2 \qquad \text{(augmented system, solve by QR)}$$
Why it matters — two weight conventions coexist
The literature writes the penalty either as $\lambda^2\|\cdot\|^2$ (Hansen's convention, used on this page throughout) or as $\alpha\|\cdot\|^2$ (ridge regression, $\alpha = \lambda^2$). Filter factors, GCV formulas, and L-curve code all silently change form between the two. Mixing them mid-derivation is a classic source of off-by-a-square bugs — fix one convention and state it.

Caveat for general $\mathbf{L}$: the SVD filter-factor formulas above hold for $\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{I}$ only. For derivative-type $\mathbf{L}$ the correct analysis uses the generalized SVD (GSVD), and components in null$(\mathbf{L})$ — the constant mode for a first-derivative $\mathbf{L}$ — pass through completely unregularized. Verify that $\mathbf{A}$ constrains them, or the "regularized" solution can still contain an arbitrarily wild unpenalized component.

8. Truncated SVD and Friends

Truncated SVD (TSVD) is the blunt-scissors alternative: keep the first $k$ terms of the SVD expansion and discard the rest.

$$\mathbf{x}_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}}{\sigma_i}\,\mathbf{v}_i \qquad\qquad f_i^{\mathrm{TSVD}} = \begin{cases} 1, & i \le k \\ 0, & i > k \end{cases} \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad f_i^{\mathrm{Tik}} = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_i^2 + \lambda^2}$$

The truncation index $k$ plays exactly the role of $\lambda$, with the correspondence $\lambda \approx \sigma_k$: Tikhonov at parameter $\lambda$ and TSVD at the $k$ where $\sigma_k \approx \lambda$ produce nearly identical solutions whenever the singular values decay fast, because the smooth roll-off and the hard cutoff then bracket the same components (Hansen 2010).

SituationNatural choiceWhy
Genuinely rank-deficient spectrum (gap)TSVD$k$ = numerical rank; truncating at the gap is unambiguous
Discrete ill-posed (gradual decay)TikhonovNo natural $k$; smooth filter with $\lambda$ chosen by Section 9
Nontrivial $\mathbf{L}$ (roughness penalty)TikhonovTSVD's analog requires the generalized SVD (truncated GSVD)
Many right-hand sides at fixed $\lambda$Tikhonov (factor once, reuse)The regime of a tactile sensor inverting at camera frame rate: $\mathbf{A}$ is fixed by skin geometry and calibration, only $\mathbf{b}$ changes per frame

Cost. A full SVD is $O(mn^2)$ — trivial for the $n \lesssim 10^3$ unknowns typical of per-frame reconstruction problems. For large 3D field identification ($n \sim 10^{5+}$) one uses iterative methods (CGLS/LSQR), which exploit semi-convergence: early iterations pick up the large-$\sigma$ components first, so the iteration count itself acts as a regularization parameter. Stop early, using the same criteria as Section 9 — running the iteration to convergence faithfully reproduces the noise-dominated naive solution.

Selective-filtering caveat. TSVD assumes the coefficient decay is roughly monotone. If the Picard plot shows an exact-coefficient spike beyond $k$, the hard cutoff deletes real signal that Tikhonov's tail would have partially kept.

Resolution: What the Filtered Solution Can Show

Apply any filtered method to noise-free data and the algebra of Section 7 gives its reconstruction of the truth as

$$\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{filtered}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i\,(\mathbf{v}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{exact}})\,\mathbf{v}_i$$

— the true solution seen through a low-pass filter. For TSVD this is exactly the orthogonal projection onto $\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_k\}$: features of $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{exact}}$ outside the first $k$ singular vectors are invisible, no matter how good the data. Since small-$\sigma$ singular vectors are the oscillatory ones (Section 5), the spatial resolution of any honest reconstruction is capped at the length scale of $\mathbf{v}_{i^*}$ — the Picard index again, now read as a resolution limit.

Key insight — regularization does not recover information
Regularization injects prior information and buys variance reduction with bias. Tikhonov systematically underestimates peaks and sharpens nothing — which matters directly when peak contact pressure is the quantity of interest. Report the bias; do not hide it. If sharp features must survive, the prior itself must change (edge-preserving or sparsity priors, Section 11), not the parameter $\lambda$.

9. Choosing the Regularization Parameter

The U-shaped error curve of Section 7 must be located blind — $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{exact}}$ is not available. Three standard criteria exist, each with an honest failure profile. None is ground truth; all three are estimators of the U's bottom.

9.1 Morozov's Discrepancy Principle

If the noise level $\delta = \|\mathbf{e}\|_2$ is known, choose $\lambda$ so the residual matches it — fitting the data below the noise level means fitting noise:

$$\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_\lambda - \mathbf{b}\|_2 = \tau\,\delta, \qquad \delta = \|\mathbf{e}\|_2, \quad \tau \gtrsim 1 \;\;(\text{e.g. } 1.05)$$

The residual norm is monotone increasing in $\lambda$, so this is a 1-D root find. Behavior: theoretically convergent as $\delta \to 0$ (Engl, Hanke & Neubauer 1996); tends to slightly over-smooth; its quality stands or falls with the $\delta$ estimate. It is the best default in engineering settings where sensor noise is characterizable.

Why it matters — $s$ is not $\delta$
The discrepancy principle consumes the noise norm $\delta = \|\mathbf{e}\|_2$, not the per-sample standard deviation $s$. For white noise, $\delta \approx s\sqrt{m}$. Confusing the two — a factor $\sqrt{m}$ — shifts the selected $\lambda$ by orders of magnitude and is a classic bug in inversion code.

9.2 The L-Curve

Plot the trade-off itself (Hansen 1992): a parametric log-log plot of $(\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_\lambda-\mathbf{b}\|_2,\; \|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x}_\lambda\|_2)$ as $\lambda$ sweeps. Under-regularized solutions populate a steep vertical branch (solution norm explodes while the residual barely improves); over-regularized ones populate a flat horizontal branch (norm barely drops while the residual grows). Pick the corner — operationalized by Hansen & O'Leary (1993) as the point of maximum curvature of the log-log curve. It needs no noise estimate and is visually informative even when not used for selection. (The figure below marks its corner with a cheaper nearest-corner heuristic; curvature-based selection is the standard implementation, and the two can disagree on a rounded L.)

Anatomy of the L-curve (log-log axes; here computed from a small synthetic ill-posed problem). The corner balances perturbation error against regularization error; the discrepancy principle instead picks the intersection with the vertical line at residual $= \tau\delta$.

Known failures, both proven, not anecdotal: for very smooth exact solutions the L-curve-selected parameter does not converge at the right rate (Hanke 1996), and the corner can degenerate when singular values decay very fast. And a computational trap: the corner is defined on the log-log curve — drawn or differentiated in linear axes, the corner moves and often disappears entirely.

Key insight — diagnostic first, selector second
Treat the L-curve as a diagnostic you always draw (its shape tells you whether regularization is doing anything sensible at all) and as a selector only second, with the Hanke caveat in mind. A beautiful corner is evidence; a degenerate corner is a warning about the problem, not about your plotting.

9.3 Generalized Cross-Validation

GCV (Golub, Heath & Wahba 1979) chooses $\lambda$ to minimize a rotation-invariant leave-one-out criterion — "how well does the model predict measurements it did not see":

$$G(\lambda) = \frac{\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_\lambda-\mathbf{b}\|_2^2}{\left[\operatorname{trace}\!\left(\mathbf{I}_m - \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}_\lambda^{\#}\right)\right]^2}, \qquad \operatorname{trace}\!\left(\mathbf{I}_m - \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}_\lambda^{\#}\right) = m - n + \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda^2}{\sigma_i^2+\lambda^2} \qquad (\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{I})$$

where $\mathbf{A}_\lambda^{\#}$ is the regularized inverse mapping $\mathbf{b} \mapsto \mathbf{x}_\lambda$. The trace costs nothing once the filter factors are known: $\operatorname{trace} = m - \sum_i f_i$. GCV is statistically optimal in expected predictive risk for white Gaussian noise and needs no noise estimate. Its failure profile: correlated noise causes drastic under-smoothing — GCV is not assumption-free; it assumes white (uncorrelated, equal-variance) noise, exactly what camera-based measurements with spatial post-processing violate (Section 4). And its minimum is often very flat, demanding careful 1-D minimization rather than eyeballing.

Practice

Sweep 50–100 log-spaced $\lambda$ values from $\sigma_n$ to $\sigma_1$, evaluate all three criteria on the sweep, and inspect the selected solutions side by side. Agreement is evidence; disagreement is diagnosis — a GCV pick far below the discrepancy pick suggests correlated noise; a wandering L-curve corner suggests fast singular-value decay. The criteria estimate the optimum, they do not certify it.

10. Nonlinear Problems: Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt

Everything so far assumed a linear forward map. Material identification breaks that assumption: the response is generally nonlinear in the parameters even when the physics is linear (Section 12). The standard treatment is iterative — linearize, solve a linear least-squares problem for the update, repeat.

Gauss–Newton. At iterate $\mathbf{m}_k$, with Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_k = \partial G/\partial \mathbf{m}$ evaluated at $\mathbf{m}_k$, the step solves the linearized problem:

$$\Delta\mathbf{m} = \arg\min_{\Delta}\,\big\|\mathbf{J}_k\,\Delta - \big(\mathbf{d} - G(\mathbf{m}_k)\big)\big\|_2^2, \qquad \mathbf{m}_{k+1} = \mathbf{m}_k + \Delta\mathbf{m}$$

Levenberg–Marquardt damps the step with a parameter $\mu$, which in normal-equations form reads

$$\left(\mathbf{J}_k^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{J}_k + \mu^2\mathbf{I}\right)\Delta\mathbf{m} = \mathbf{J}_k^\mathsf{T}\big(\mathbf{d} - G(\mathbf{m}_k)\big)$$

— formally a Tikhonov term with $\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{I}$ applied to the update. Large $\mu$ shortens the step toward gradient descent; small $\mu$ recovers Gauss–Newton. In practice $\mu$ is shrunk as iterations succeed and grown when they fail.

The connection to the rest of the module is direct: the Jacobian plays the role of $\mathbf{A}$, so each iteration is a (regularized) linear least-squares problem, and the SVD of $\mathbf{J}$ at the solution governs local identifiability — small singular values of $\mathbf{J}$ mark parameter combinations the data barely constrains. The Section 4 warning applies verbatim: forming $\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{J}$ squares the condition number; solve the augmented system $[\mathbf{J};\,\mu\mathbf{I}]$ by QR instead.

Key insight — damping is not regularization
LM's $\mu$ stabilizes the step, not the problem: it is driven toward zero as the iteration converges, so at convergence you are minimizing the plain data-misfit functional. If the underlying problem is ill-posed, the converged answer is still noise-dominated. An ill-posed nonlinear problem needs an explicit regularization term in the objective itself — $\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^2 + \lambda^2\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x}\|^2$ with $\lambda$ chosen as in Section 9 — on top of whatever damping the optimizer uses.

11. The Bayesian View: Regularization as a Prior

Reframe the whole construction statistically (Kaipio & Somersalo 2005; Calvetti & Somersalo 2018). Model the noise as Gaussian, $\mathbf{e}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\mathbf{I})$, and encode prior belief as a Gaussian prior on the unknown. Bayes' theorem multiplies likelihood and prior; taking $-\log$ of the posterior and dropping constants yields exactly the Tikhonov functional.

Tikhonov = Gaussian MAP
Under three assumptions — linear forward map, Gaussian noise $\mathbf{e}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\mathbf{I})$, and Gaussian prior $\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_0,\gamma^2(\mathbf{L}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{L})^{-1})$ with $\mathbf{L}$ of full column rank — the maximum a posteriori estimate is the Tikhonov solution: $$\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{MAP}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{x}}\left\{\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 + \lambda^2\|\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)\|_2^2\right\}, \qquad \lambda^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{\gamma^2}$$ Drop any one assumption and the equivalence breaks.

Three consequences worth internalizing:

  • $\lambda$ stops being a fudge factor. It is the noise-to-prior variance ratio — "how much do I trust the sensor relative to my prior about the field". A strong prior (small $\gamma$) means large $\lambda$.
  • $\mathbf{L}$ is a statement about spatial correlation. A first-derivative $\mathbf{L}$ says increments are independent Gaussians — smooth fields are a-priori probable. That is precisely right for a pressure field under a smooth indenter, and wrong (over-smoothing) for sharp contact edges — which motivates non-Gaussian alternatives: a Laplace prior gives an $\ell_1$ penalty, a sparsity-promoting MAP that is no longer linear in $\mathbf{b}$.
  • The posterior comes with error bars. In the linear-Gaussian setting the posterior is fully Gaussian, so regularized inversion silently carries an uncertainty estimate (details in the collapsible below).

Caveats, stated bluntly: MAP = Tikhonov requires linear forward map and Gaussian noise and Gaussian prior — Laplace/sparsity priors, positivity constraints, or nonlinear models all break the equivalence. And the posterior quantifies uncertainty only within the assumed model: model error — wrong $E$, wrong geometry, the bias term $\mathbf{b}$ of Section 2's spine — is not included in those error bars.

Full derivation, posterior covariance, and where the field went after 2018

Likelihood times prior:

$$\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{b}) \;\propto\; \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_2^2\right)\,\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2\gamma^2}\|\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)\|_2^2\right)$$

Taking $-\log$ and multiplying through by $2\sigma^2$ gives the Tikhonov functional with $\lambda^2 = \sigma^2/\gamma^2$. Because the exponent is quadratic, the posterior is Gaussian with mean equal to the MAP estimate and covariance

$$\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{post}} = \sigma^2\left(\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A} + \lambda^2\mathbf{L}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{L}\right)^{-1}$$

The diagonal gives a per-unknown posterior standard deviation, and the directions of large posterior variance are exactly the small-$\sigma_i$ singular directions the data cannot see — the Bayesian restatement of Sections 2 and 5.

Unknown hyperparameters $\sigma$, $\gamma$ lead to hierarchical Bayes / evidence maximization, conceptually adjacent to GCV. Modern practice replaces hand-built Gaussian priors with learned generative priors and samples the posterior instead of maximizing it — e.g. conditional score-based diffusion models for inverse elasticity problems (Dasgupta et al. 2025) — but the likelihood-plus-prior scaffolding of this section, and the ill-posedness it compensates, remain exactly as taught here.

12. A Taxonomy of Inverse Problems in Solid Mechanics

Organize the field by which ingredient of the boundary-value problem is unknown, everything else given (Bonnet & Constantinescu 2005) — the solid-mechanics instantiation of Section 2's spine.

Four classes of inverse problem in solid mechanics, organized by the unknown ingredient (Bonnet & Constantinescu 2005). Shape sensing — iFEM's home — is the benign member of class 3.

Class 1 — Load Identification

Geometry, material, and boundary conditions known; applied forces or pressures unknown. For linear elasticity the response is linear in the loads, so discretization gives

$$\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{f} + \mathbf{e}, \qquad A_{ij} = \text{response at sensor } i \text{ to unit load } j$$

with $\mathbf{A}$ an influence/transfer matrix assembled from the FE model (columns = responses to unit loads) — directly a linear discrete ill-posed problem to which Sections 4–9 apply verbatim. Ill-posedness worsens with sensor-to-load distance (Saint-Venant smoothing, Section 1). Dynamic variants add time deconvolution on top; Liu et al. (2022) review the frequency- and time-domain machinery, the ill-posedness handling, and the ML variants. Contact-force-map reconstruction in tactile skin is squarely this class.

Class 2 — Material / Parameter Identification

Constitutive parameters or spatial modulus fields unknown. The response is generally nonlinear in the parameters even when the physics is linear, so the problem is solved iteratively — each Gauss–Newton step (Section 10) is a regularized linear least-squares problem in the parameter update. For full-field data the method families are FEMU (finite element model updating), the constitutive equation gap, the virtual fields method, the equilibrium gap, and the reciprocity gap (Avril et al. 2008). Römer et al. (2025) recast these as reduced (a forward solver in the loop) versus all-at-once (field and parameters jointly unknown) formulations, with PINN-type approaches in the all-at-once family. Elastography / elasticity imaging is the field-identification version of this class.

Class 3 — Shape, Configuration, and Defect Identification

Unknown geometry. The hard direction: cracks, voids, inclusions — nonlinear, handled with adjoint fields, level sets, and topological derivatives. The benign direction: shape sensing — reconstructing the deformed configuration from discrete strain measurements. This is where iFEM lives (Module 4): a variational least-squares state-reconstruction problem, much better conditioned than the other classes — but still an inverse problem, with observability that depends on sensor placement.

Class 4 — Boundary-Condition Identification

Unknown tractions or displacements on an inaccessible boundary, from overdetermined data on the accessible part — the Cauchy problem of elasticity. Severely (exponentially) ill-posed; regularization is indispensable, not optional (Bonnet & Constantinescu 2005).

Key insight
After discretization and linearization, every class funnels through the same regularized least-squares machinery of this module. What changes between classes is the conditioning of the resulting operator and the prior that is defensible — not the mathematics of the solve.

13. Practical Workflow: Diagnosing and Solving Your Inverse Problem

A field recipe consolidating the module. Follow it in order; each step is cheap compared to debugging its omission.

  1. Scale first. Non-dimensionalize rows (divide by the sensor noise std per channel — whitening, Section 4) and columns (characteristic magnitudes of the unknowns). Conditioning and the SVD are not scale-invariant: a $\kappa = 10^9$ that drops to $10^3$ after sensible scaling was a units problem, not an ill-posed problem.
  2. Compute the SVD of the scaled $\mathbf{A}$ and classify the spectrum (Section 5): flat → ordinary least squares, stop here; gap → rank-deficient, TSVD at the gap; gradual decay → discrete ill-posed, continue.
  3. Picard plot with real data (Section 6); read off the crossing index $i^*$ = number of recoverable components. If $i^*$ is far below the number of unknowns, consider re-parameterizing coarser — fewer basis functions for the pressure field — instead of fighting with heavy regularization.
  4. Choose $\mathbf{L}$ from prior physics (Section 7): identity for magnitude, gradient/Laplacian for smoothness; check that null$(\mathbf{L})$ is observable through $\mathbf{A}$.
  5. Sweep $\lambda$ over 50–100 log-spaced values spanning $[\sigma_n, \sigma_1]$; evaluate discrepancy (if $\delta$ is known), L-curve corner, and GCV (Section 9); compare the selected $\lambda$'s and inspect the corresponding solutions side by side.
  6. Validate honestly. (a) Residual check: after subtracting the fit, the residual should look like sensor noise — white, correct magnitude. A structured residual means model error (the bias term $\mathbf{b}$ of Section 2's spine). (b) Withheld data: hold out sensors or whole loading cases from the fit and score the reconstruction on predicting them — a solution that only explains the data it was fitted to has explained nothing. (c) No inverse crimes (box below). (d) Report the bias: Tikhonov solutions systematically underestimate peaks and sharpen nothing — state this wherever peak values (e.g. peak contact pressure) are the quantity of interest.
  7. For repeated real-time inversion (fixed $\mathbf{A}$, streaming $\mathbf{b}$): precompute the SVD, or the QR/Cholesky factorization of the augmented system at the chosen $\lambda$; the per-frame cost drops to two matrix–vector products, $O(mn)$.
Definition — Inverse Crime
Validating an inversion pipeline on synthetic data generated with the same discretization, model, and noise assumptions used inside the inversion. The test then flatters the method — the only error source left is the one your regularizer is tuned to remove. Generate test data on a finer or different mesh, with realistic (possibly correlated) noise, or the reconstruction quality you report is fiction.
